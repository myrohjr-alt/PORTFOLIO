O.A.S.I.S.

A $40 air quality monitor that placed 7th in the nation

Role: Sensor electronics and enclosure Year: 2026

My team built O.A.S.I.S. for the Technology Student Association’s Engineering Design competition. The prompt asked for a tool for scientific discovery. We picked air quality. The reference instruments the EPA uses run $15,000 to $70,000, which puts real measurements out of reach for a neighborhood that wants its own numbers. Ours costs about $40 a node. I handled the sensor electronics and the enclosure across four rounds of prototypes.

It placed 7th in the nation at the TSA national conference.

Sensors
PMS5003 particulate, MICS6814 NO₂/CO/NH₃, BME280 environmental
Controller
ESP32, I²C and UART, on-device OLED diagnostics
Board
Custom PCB laid out in KiCad, toner-transfer etched in-house
Enclosure
PETG, four revisions, louvered side venting, snap-fit cover
Cost
Under $5 per board; ~$40 per node
Result
7th in the nation, TSA Engineering Design

The unit in the field

Enclosure, four revisions

I modeled the enclosure in CAD and rebuilt it four times. Every version failed at something first.

V1 used a press-fit chassis. It cracked when I drilled it, and I couldn’t add vents without breaking the weather seal. V2 moved to an isogrid box and still let water in during rain testing. V3 put the vents on the bottom, angled so water couldn’t climb in while warm air could still rise out. V4 moved them again, to louvered slats on the sides, and added a top cover that snaps off by hand instead of needing a screwdriver. It came out about 20% smaller than V3.

I tested every version three ways: mist spray, a freezer, and direct sun. Then I put units out to record. One by a road, one at a house, one on a school campus.

Electronics

Three sensors do the measuring. A PMS5003 counts particles using laser scatter. A MICS6814 array reads NO₂, CO, and NH₃. A BME280 handles temperature, humidity, and pressure. All three report to an ESP32.

The first build lived on a breadboard. I moved it onto a PCB I laid out in KiCad, then made the boards myself with toner transfer and ferric chloride. Tightening the trace spacing across revisions shrank the board by roughly 60%, and each one costs under $5 to make.

Power was mine too. For field testing, a 9V supply steps down to a clean 5V rail for the ESP32. The production version runs off AC line power instead.
